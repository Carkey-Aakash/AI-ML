Correlation & Covariance¶

1. What is covariance and what does it measure in a dataset?¶

Covariance is a statistical measure that describes how two variables change together. It tells us whether an increase in one variable tends to be associated with an increase or decrease in another variable.

What covariance tells us:¶

Positive covariance (>0)

  • When X increases, Y tends to increase.
  • Example: Hours studied vs. marks scored.

Negative covariance (<0)

  • When X increases, Y tends to decrease.
  • Example: Hours spent on TV vs. exam marks.

Zero covariance (≈ 0):

  • The variables are not linearly related; changes in one do not predict changes in the other.

Important points:¶

Covariance only measures direction, not the strength of the relationship.

2. What is correlation and how is it different from covariance?¶

Correlation vs Covariance

1. Covariance

Definition:
Covariance is a statistical measure that indicates the direction of the linear relationship between two variables. It tells whether two variables tend to increase or decrease together.

Interpretation:

  • Positive covariance (>0): Both variables increase or decrease together.
  • Negative covariance (<0): One variable increases while the other decreases.
  • Covariance ≈ 0: Little or no linear relationship.

Limitations:

  • Its value depends on the units of X and Y, making it hard to compare across datasets.

2. Correlation

Definition:
Correlation measures both the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables. It is a standardized version of covariance and is unitless, making it easier to interpret.

Interpretation:

  • r = 1: Perfect positive linear relationship
  • r = -1: Perfect negative linear relationship
  • r ≈ 0: No linear relationship

3. Key Differences

Feature Covariance Correlation
Measures Direction of relationship Direction + Strength
Scale Depends on units Unitless (standardized)
Range (-∞, +∞) [-1, 1]
Interpretability Hard to compare Easy to compare across datasets

3. What is the range of correlation values and what do they indicate?¶

r=+1 (Perfect Positive Correlation): A correlation value of +1 indicates a perfect positive linear relationship between two variables. This means that as one variable increases, the other variable increases in exactly the same proportion every time. All data points lie on a straight line sloping upward. Such a relationship is rare in real-world data but can be seen in mathematically related variables, such as temperature measured in Celsius and Fahrenheit.

0<𝑟<+1 (Positive Correlation): A correlation value between 0 and +1 shows a positive relationship, meaning both variables tend to increase together, though not perfectly. As the value moves closer to +1, the relationship becomes stronger. For example, as study hours increase, exam scores generally increase, but not in an exact pattern for every student.

𝑟=0 (No Linear Correlation): A correlation value of 0 indicates no linear relationship between the two variables. Changes in one variable do not predict changes in the other in a straight-line manner. However, this does not mean the variables are completely unrelated; there may still be a non-linear relationship present that correlation does not capture.

−1<𝑟<0 (Negative Correlation): A correlation value between 0 and −1 represents a negative relationship, where one variable tends to decrease as the other increases. The closer the value is to −1, the stronger the inverse relationship. For instance, as the speed of a vehicle increases, the time required to reach a destination generally decreases.

𝑟=−1 (Perfect Negative Correlation): A correlation value of −1 indicates a perfect negative linear relationship. As one variable increases, the other decreases in exact proportion every time. All data points lie on a straight line sloping downward. Like perfect positive correlation, this is uncommon in real-world datasets but can occur in strictly defined mathematical relationships.

4. What does a positive covariance mean?¶

A positive covariance means that two variables tend to move in the same direction. When one variable increases, the other variable also tends to increase, and when one decreases, the other tends to decrease. It indicates a direct relationship between the variables, but it does not tell how strong the relationship is—only that they change together.

5. What does a negative correlation represent in real-world data?¶

A negative correlation in real-world data represents an inverse relationship between two variables, where an increase in one variable is associated with a decrease in the other. For example, as the number of hours spent watching television increases, students’ study time or academic performance may decrease. This relationship shows that the variables move in opposite directions, though the correlation does not imply that one directly causes the other.

6. Why is covariance not normalized?¶

Covariance is not normalized because its value depends on the units and scale of the variables being measured. Since it is calculated using the original units of both variables, changing the units (for example, meters to centimeters) will change the covariance value, even though the underlying relationship stays the same. This makes covariance difficult to interpret and compare across different datasets. Normalization would remove unit dependence, which is why correlation is used instead it standardizes covariance by dividing it by the product of the variables’ standard deviations.

7. Can correlation exist without causation? Explain with an example.¶

Yes, correlation can exist without causation. This means that two variables may show a relationship or pattern together, but one does not directly cause the other.

Example: The number of mobile phones sold and the number of engineering graduates increase over the years. These two variables are positively correlated because both increase with time, but mobile phone sales do not cause more students to graduate as engineers. The apparent relationship exists due to a common underlying factor-population growth and technological advancement not because one causes the other.

8. What does a correlation value close to 0 indicate?¶

A correlation value close to 0 indicates that there is little to no linear relationship between the two variables in the dataset. This means that changes in one variable do not consistently correspond to increases or decreases in the other variable in a predictable way. However, it is important to note that a correlation near zero does not necessarily imply that the variables are completely unrelated; they may still have a non-linear relationship that correlation does not capture. In short, a correlation value close to zero suggests the absence of a meaningful linear association between the variables

def correlation_rounded(data1, data2):
    """
    Compute Pearson correlation coefficient between two lists,
    handling missing values, and return rounded to 2 decimal places.
    """
    # Remove pairs where either value is None
    clean_pairs = [(x, y) for x, y in zip(data1, data2) if x is not None and y is not None]
    
    if len(clean_pairs) < 2:
        raise ValueError("Not enough valid data to compute correlation.")
    
    # Unpack the cleaned pairs
    data1_clean, data2_clean = zip(*clean_pairs)
    
    # Compute means
    mean1 = sum(data1_clean) / len(data1_clean)
    mean2 = sum(data2_clean) / len(data2_clean)
    
    # Compute covariance
    cov = sum((x - mean1) * (y - mean2) for x, y in zip(data1_clean, data2_clean)) / (len(data1_clean) - 1)
    
    # Compute standard deviations
    std1 = (sum((x - mean1)**2 for x in data1_clean) / (len(data1_clean) - 1)) ** 0.5
    std2 = (sum((y - mean2)**2 for y in data2_clean) / (len(data2_clean) - 1)) ** 0.5
    
    # Compute correlation
    corr = cov / (std1 * std2)
    
    # Round to 2 decimal places
    return round(corr, 2)


# Example usage
hours_studied = [2, 4, 6, 8, 10]
exam_scores = [50, 60, 65, None, 85]

result = correlation_rounded(hours_studied, exam_scores)
print("Correlation (rounded to 2 decimals):", result)

# Function to remove outliers using IQR
def remove_outliers(data):
    data=[x for x in data if x is not None]
    q1 = sorted(data)[len(data)//4]
    q3 = sorted(data)[3*len(data)//4]
    iqr = q3 - q1
    lower = q1 - 1.5*iqr
    upper = q3 + 1.5*iqr
    return [x for x in data if lower <= x <= upper]

def load_numerical_columns(filepath):
    numerical_data = {
        "longitude": [], "latitude": [], "housing_median_age": [],
        "total_rooms": [], "population": [], "households": [],
        "median_income": [], "median_house_value": []
    }

    with open(filepath, "r", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        reader = csv.DictReader(f)
        for row in reader:
            for key in numerical_data:
                val = row[key]
                numerical_data[key].append(float(val) if val != "" else None)

    return numerical_data


# -----------------------------
# Basic statistics (YOUR STYLE)
# -----------------------------
def mean(data):
    return sum(data) / len(data)

def std_dev(data):
    m = mean(data)
    var = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in data) / (len(data) - 1)
    return var ** 0.5

def covariance(data1, data2):
    m1 = mean(data1)
    m2 = mean(data2)

    cov_sum = 0
    for x, y in zip(data1, data2):
        cov_sum += (x - m1) * (y - m2)

    return cov_sum / (len(data1) - 1)

def correlation(x, y):
    return covariance(x, y) / (std_dev(x) * std_dev(y))


# -----------------------------
# Remove None values pairwise
# -----------------------------
def clean_pairs(x, y):
    clean_x, clean_y = [], []
    for a, b in zip(x, y):
        if a is not None and b is not None:
            clean_x.append(a)
            clean_y.append(b)
    return clean_x, clean_y

20. Explain how to interpret dark vs light colors in a heatmap.

Heatmap basics

A correlation heatmap uses colors to show strength and direction of correlation between two variables.

Each cell in the heatmap = correlation coefficient

r ranges from -1 to +1

r value Meaning 1 Perfect positive correlation 0 No correlation -1 Perfect negative correlation

Color interpretation Heatmaps usually use a color scale like coolwarm:

Dark red / warm colors → high positive correlation (r close to +1)

Dark blue / cool colors → high negative correlation (r close to -1)

Light / white / pale colors → weak or no correlation (r near 0)

22. Explain why diagonal values in correlation matrix are always 1.

In a correlation matrix, the diagonal values are always 1 because each variable is being compared with itself. Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables, and when a variable is compared with itself, the relationship is perfectly linear. Using the Pearson correlation formula, the covariance of a variable with itself equals its variance, and dividing by the product of its standard deviations gives 1. Intuitively, this makes sense because a variable is always perfectly proportional to itself, so the correlation is maximum. These diagonal values serve as a reference, showing that each variable has perfect self-correlation, while the off-diagonal values represent correlations between different variables.

import csv
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

# -----------------------------
# Load numerical columns
# -----------------------------
def load_numerical_columns(filepath):
    numerical_data = {
        "longitude": [], "latitude": [], "housing_median_age": [],
        "total_rooms": [], "population": [], "households": [],
        "median_income": [], "median_house_value": []
    }

    with open(filepath, "r", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        reader = csv.DictReader(f)
        for row in reader:
            for key in numerical_data:
                val = row[key]
                numerical_data[key].append(float(val) if val != "" else None)

    return numerical_data

# -----------------------------
# Basic statistics
# -----------------------------
def mean(data):
    return sum(data) / len(data)

def std_dev(data):
    m = mean(data)
    var = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in data) / (len(data) - 1)
    return var ** 0.5

def covariance(data1, data2):
    m1 = mean(data1)
    m2 = mean(data2)
    cov_sum = sum((x - m1) * (y - m2) for x, y in zip(data1, data2))
    return cov_sum / (len(data1) - 1)

def correlation(x, y):
    return covariance(x, y) / (std_dev(x) * std_dev(y))

# -----------------------------
# Remove None values pairwise
# -----------------------------
def clean_pairs(x, y):
    clean_x, clean_y = [], []
    for a, b in zip(x, y):
        if a is not None and b is not None:
            clean_x.append(a)
            clean_y.append(b)
    return clean_x, clean_y

# -----------------------------
# Build covariance and correlation matrices
# -----------------------------
data = load_numerical_columns("housing.csv")
columns = list(data.keys())

cov_matrix = []
corr_matrix = []

for col1 in columns:
    cov_row = []
    corr_row = []
    for col2 in columns:
        x, y = clean_pairs(data[col1], data[col2])
        cov_val = covariance(x, y)
        corr_val = correlation(x, y)
        cov_row.append(round(cov_val, 2))
        corr_row.append(round(corr_val, 2))
    cov_matrix.append(cov_row)
    corr_matrix.append(corr_row)

# -----------------------------
# Plot side-by-side heatmaps
# -----------------------------
plt.figure(figsize=(17, 7))

# Covariance heatmap
plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
sns.heatmap(
    cov_matrix,
    annot=True,
    xticklabels=columns,
    yticklabels=columns,
    cmap="coolwarm"
)
plt.title("Covariance Matrix (Housing Dataset)")

# Correlation heatmap
plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
sns.heatmap(
    corr_matrix,
    annot=True,
    xticklabels=columns,
    yticklabels=columns,
    cmap="coolwarm"
)
plt.title("Correlation Matrix (Housing Dataset)")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

When correlation can be misleading

Correlation measures linear relationships between two variables, but it does not always tell the full story. Here are common scenarios where correlation can be misleading:

1.Non-linear relationships If two variables are related non-linearly, correlation may be close to 0, even if they have a strong relationship.

Example: 𝑦=𝑥^2

Visualization: Scatter plot shows a parabola, clearly a relationship, but Pearson correlation ≈ 0 because positive and negative deviations cancel out.

2️.Outliers A few extreme values can inflate or deflate correlation, giving a misleading impression of the relationship. Example: Most points are uncorrelated, but one outlier creates a strong correlation.

Visualization: Scatter plot with one extreme point shows an apparent trend that doesn’t reflect most data.
